# Longchenpa — the facts to be spoken

## 1. Previous lives

1. Longchenpa is held to be an incarnation of Princess Pemasal, daughter of King Trisong Detsen.
2. Guru Rinpoche gave her the mind-mandate transmission of the Khandro Nyingtik, sealing it within her awareness, and the secret name Pema Ledreltsal. (IMAGE 1 — the princess receives the transmission)
3. She died at the age of eight.
4. When the king asked why a child of such merit had to die so young, Padmasambhava told how she had once been a bee that stung one of the four brothers building the Great Stupa of Boudhanath.
5. After many incarnations she was reborn as the tertön Pema Ledreltsal, who revealed the Khandro Nyingtik as terma but decoded it only in part.
6. He was then reborn as Longchenpa, who completed the work. Longchenpa is the \*\*immediate\*\* reincarnation of the tertön, and only an \*\*indirect\*\* incarnation of the princess.

## 2. Birth

1. He was born in 1308 at Tötrong in the Tra valley of Yoru, in southern Central Tibet.
2. His father was Tenpasung, a tantric yogi.
3. Before his conception his mother dreamt of a great lion with the sun and moon on its forehead, whose light illuminated the three realms before dissolving into her body.  (IMAGE 2 — the mother's dream of the lion)
4. At the birth the protectress Remati (Palden Lhamo; wrathful Saraswati) took the infant in her arms, vowed to protect him, returned him to his mother, and vanished. (IMAGE 2A — Remati guarding the door)
5. In his own autobiography he writes that Remati gave him close protection, and that this was seen in physical form.

## 3. Childhood and study

1. From the age of five he studied with his father and learned to read.
2. At seven he received the Eight Sadhanas and the practices of Hayagrīva, Vajrakīla and Guru Rinpoche.
3. At nine he memorised the Prajñāpāramitā sūtras in twenty thousand and in eight thousand lines, reciting them each, a hundred times. (IMAGE 3 — the boy reciting by lamplight)
4. At twelve he saw saṃsāra as a nest of poisonous snakes, a pit of fire, a continent of demons. (IMAGE 3A — the snakes and the fire in the hollow)
5. At twelve he was ordained a novice at Samye.
6. By fourteen he had written a commentary on the Vinaya and could teach it.
7. At sixteen he received the major Sarma cycles: Lamdré, the Six Yogas and Cakrasaṃvara. He also received  Chöd.
8. At nineteen he entered Sangphu Neuthang for six years, studying Maitreya's Five Treatises, Dharmakīrti's logic, Madhyamaka and Prajñāpāramitā, alongside the Third Karmapa.
9. He studied with more than twenty masters across all schools, and was known as Samye Lungmangpa, the one from Samye with vast scripture.
10. After devoted practice of her sādhana, Sarasvatī — Yangchenma, goddess of learning and melody — appeared to him in a vision and held him in the palm of her hand for seven days, showed him the four continents and Mount Meru, and prophesied that he would become a great master. (IMAGE 3B — held in Sarasvatī's palm above the continents) \[Age disputed: one account says sixteen; others place it in his Sangphu years, from nineteen. No source calls it a dream.\]

## 4. Leaving Sangphu

1. Sangphu was dominated by students from Kham, and he came into conflict with a group of these scholars, whom he found sectarian and of poor moral character.
2. He was also under constant provocation from fellow monks.
3. He left in 1334, at the age of twenty-six. (IMAGE 4A — walking out of Sangphu)
4. His disgust produced a thirty-line satirical poem, the Ka kha sum cu, with one line for each letter of the Tibetan alphabet, in nine-syllable verse. It mocks the Khampa students as lawless raiders driven by greed, anger and arrogance.
5. The story that a monk carried it back and pinned it up at the college may be a later literary borrowing. \[Say "the story goes".\]
6. He was disheartened and weary of saṃsāra, and felt sorrow for the suffering of the world.
7. He left with bare necessities, begging along the road, and meditated eight months in a cave in complete darkness.
8. At dawn Tārā appeared, blessed him, and told him he would meet Rigdzin Kumārādza and become his disciple.

## 5. Kumārādza

1. At twenty-seven he found Rigdzin Kumārādza, 1266 to 1343, in the highlands of the upper Yartö Kyam valley, and recognised him on sight as Vimalamitra in person. \[Some sources say twenty-nine.\] (IMAGE 4B — the summons in the yak-hair tent)
2. About seventy disciples were living there in temporary shelters.
3. Kumārādza kept no fixed home, in order to arouse disillusionment with the world and prevent attachment to any place. In nine months they moved camp nine times.
4. Longchenpa had no money for the customary offering and did not wish to stay on those terms. The master sent word that he need not worry about offerings.
5. The holy lama told him that a wondrous sign had appeared, a bird, and that it referred to him. \[The autobiography does not call this a dream, though English accounts often do.\] (IMAGE 4D — the lama pointing out the bird)
6. Kumārādza said he would rather teach him than all the others who had come to listen.
7. In his second year he received the three divisions, Semde, Longde and Mengagde, including the Nyingtik, at the Shampo glacier on Yarlha Shampo, and was made holder of the lineage. (IMAGE 4C — Padmasambhava subduing the yak-god of the mountain)
8. There he lived on flour and some twenty-one rinchen rilbu (precious pills), with a worn sack serving as seat, blanket and shelter. (IMAGE 4 — the sack on the glacier)
9. In one spring he moved nine times between desolate valleys and lived two months on very little food, and he vowed to endure such hardship for three years.
10. He remained with Kumārādza about two years.
11. In retreat at Chimphu and other solitudes he had visions of Khecara and of teaching ḍākinīs, and in charnel grounds the protectors Ekajaṭī, Rāhula, Vajrasādhu and Zhanglön Dorje Düdül appeared to him.

## 6. The visions and the names

1. At thirty-two he gave the Vima Nyingtik empowerment for the first time at Shuksep.
2. His yogi disciple Öser Kocha found a copy of the Khandro Nyingtik.
3. Though he was himself the reincarnation of the tertön Pema Ledreltsal, he sought formal transmission from the tertön's chief disciple, Shuk Gyalse Lekpa, so that both the terma and the kama lineages would remain unbroken. \[One link only: Pema Ledreltsal entrusted his yellow scrolls to Lekpa, who transmitted them to Longchenpa.\]
4. A black woman appeared, offered him a Khandro Nyingtik text, and vanished. He understood this as an exhortation from the goddess Sokdrupma — not a new source for the cycle, which he already held from Lekpa, but a prompting to teach it — and he resolved to give it at Chimphu.
5. At thirty-three he taught the cycle to eight fortunate men and women at Samye Chimphu. Ekajaṭī spoke through a yoginī, flowers fell, rainbow light filled the sky, and the participants danced in bliss. Ekajaṭī oversaw the ritual in detail — she insisted on a peacock feather, and corrected his pronunciation so that he chanted as she did, in the manner of ḍākinī language. (IMAGE 5 — the empowerment in the cave)
6. Guru Rinpoche and Yeshe Tsogyal (Padmasambava's consort, consider also the mother of Tibetan Buddhism) empowered him and gave him the names Drimé Özer and Dorje Ziji Tsal, authorising him to compose treatises, with Vimalamitra at his right and Vajravārāhī at his left.
7. At the Jowo Śākyamuni in Lhasa, light came from the tuft of hair between the Buddha's brows and dissolved into him, and he recalled past lives as a scholar at Vulture Peak and in Khotan. (IMAGE 5A — light from the brows of the Jowo)
8. He received the title Künkhyen Chöje, All-Knowing King of Dharma, after humbling the pride of learned scholars at Lhasa and Ramoche.

## 7. Gangri Tökar and Zhai

1. On the advice of the protectress Yudrönma he settled at Orgyen Dzong on Gangri Tökar, White Skull Snow Mountain, which became his main seat.
2. There he taught by day and received ḍākinī instruction by night, composing the Seven Treasuries, the Trilogy of Rest, and more than two hundred and fifty treatises in all. (IMAGE 6 — writing by lamplight at Gangri Tökar)
3. He had visions of Samantabhadra, Vimalamitra, Kumārādza and Orgyen Pema Gyalpo, and remained in continuous thögal awareness.
4. An attendant is said to have seen him order Viṣṇu to prepare his ink.
5. Vimalamitra taught him in a pure vision and entrusted him with the Vima Nyingtik, on which he wrote the Lama Yangtik in thirty-five treatises.
6. Gompa Kün-rik of Drikung funded the restoration of Zhai temple in Uru and became his disciple. Longchenpa turned him from warfare to practice.
7. During the work he manifested as wrathful Padmasambhava and subdued a hostile force released from buried objects, exactly as Vimalamitra had prophesied.
8. At the consecration many saw him appear as Samantabhadra, and he beheld Śākyamuni, Maitreya and the Sixteen Arhats. (IMAGE 6A — the three mirrors)
9. Maitreya prophesied his final birth as the buddha Rirab Marmé Gyaltsen in the realm Pema Tsekpa.
10. He is counted, with Sakya Pandita and Tsongkhapa, as one of the three great emanations of Manjushri to have taught in Central Tibet.

## 8. Bhutan

1. From 1346 Central Tibet was in civil war. Longchenpa's patron led Drikung, which fought against Changchub Gyaltsen of Phagmodru; suspecting an alliance, he sent forces to kill Longchenpa, who escaped by miraculous means to Bumthang in Bhutan with close disciples and was there by the mid-1350s. (IMAGE 7 — the hermitage in Bumthang)
2. He founded eight hermitages, chief among them Tharpaling, and concealed termas there.
3. He brought forth springs, left handprints and footprints in rock, and bound local spirits to service.
4. He gave up his monastic vows, took as consort Kyipa of Bhutan, and had a son and a daughter.
5. His son, Tulku Drakpa Özer, became a lineage holder.
6. He taught Dzogchen to more than a thousand practitioners at Nyepu Shuksep, and gave Nyingtik empowerments to over three thousand near Trok Orgyen.
7. He avoided large institutions and kept to caves, never accepted payment for teaching, used offerings for nothing but the Dharma, and showed particular kindness to the poor.

## 9. The passing

1. At fifty-six he dictated his testament, Drima Mepé Ö, Immaculate Radiance, comparing death to a traveller setting out on an open road and saying his joy at it far exceeds a merchant's who has gained a whole ocean of wealth. \[The Water Hare month falls in early 1364, which is why both 1363 and 1364 appear in print.\]
2. Passing through Samye to Chimphu, he announced that he would leave his illusory body in that charnel ground, and began to show signs of sickness, yet continued to teach the crowds who gathered.
3. On the sixteenth day of the twelfth month he performed an elaborate offering ceremony, gave a last teaching on impermanence, and urged his disciples to practise trekchö and thögal.
4. For anyone who struggled with his teachings he recommended the Lama Yangtik, saying it would be like a wish-fulfilling jewel.
5. On the eighteenth day, seated in the dharmakāya posture, his mind dissolved into absolute dharma-space. Those present felt the earth tremble and heard roaring sounds.
6. His body was kept undisturbed for twenty-five days, during which a tent of rainbows arched constantly across the sky. (IMAGE 8 — rainbows over the winter valley)
7. In the coldest month of the Tibetan year the earth grew warm, the ice melted, wild roses bloomed, and fragrance filled the air.
8. At the cremation the earth trembled three times and a loud sound was heard seven times.
9. Countless five-coloured ringsel and five kinds of large relics came from the bones, read as the sign of the five wisdoms and the five kāyas.
10. His heart, skull, tongue and eyes remained unburned, and fell into the laps of his disciples, signifying enlightened body, speech and mind.
11. His principal disciples were counted as three learned and accomplished ones, five sons of his enlightened mind, and four spiritual friends.

## 10. After him

1. His incarnation line continues into Pema Lingpa, 1450 to 1521, the great Bhutanese tertön.
2. Four centuries after his death, Jigme Lingpa had three visions of Longchen Rabjam's wisdom body and received the blessings of his body, speech and mind.
3. From this came the Longchen Nyingtik, today among the most widely practised cycles in the Nyingma tradition.
